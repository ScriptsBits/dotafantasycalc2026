Before anything else, here is exactly what we are scoring

Every number in this script is built on one specific reading of the payout, and if that reading is wrong then a lot of the arithmetic moves. So let us pin it down first.

You field three players: a Core, a Mid, and a Support. Each of them carries a banner, and each banner holds three emblems. An emblem is just a stat with a points-per-unit price attached. Creep Score pays 3 points per last hit, a Roshan kill pays 1,172, a smoke pays 293, and so on.

The banners are colour locked. Core gets Red, Green, Red. Mid gets Red, Blue, Green. Support gets Blue, Green, Blue. You do not choose the colours, you choose which stat goes in each colour slot, and rerolling costs tokens.

We are scoring one series, exactly two games. In each game we take the average across the players in that group, so the Support banner is the average of both supports in game one, plus the average of both supports in game two. Same for Core, which averages position 1 and position 3. Mid is a single player, so nothing to average. Two games, added together. That is the whole number.

Why the rule matters this much

If the contest averaged your games instead of summing a fixed two, then the number of games your team plays would not matter at all. If it summed every game of a long run, the number of games would matter more than every emblem decision put together. Fixing it at one series of two games puts us in between, and segment 5 is entirely about what that does to team selection.

01
Method
How the prediction works
ON SCREEN: build Figure 2 line by line as each idea is named. Then Figure 3, one quadrant at a time.

Four ideas do all the work, and none of them is complicated

People assume a projection like this is a black box. It is not. It is a weighted average with three corrections bolted on, and each correction exists because a specific naive version failed a specific test.

Start with the obvious thing, then fix what breaks

The naive method is: take a player's recent games, average their Watcher count, call that the projection. That fails in four ways, so here are the four fixes.

  • Weight recent games more heavily. Every game is weighted by 0.5 raised to the power of its age divided by 45 days. A game from six weeks ago counts half as much as one from this week. That means an 18-month window is not an 18-month average: the last two months carry most of the weight, and the long tail is only there to sharpen the estimate. Across the 80 listed players, a median of 371 raw games collapses to a median of 99 effective games.
  • Predict the player's gap from the field, not the player's raw number. Patches change absolute levels. A creep score of 300 means something different this patch than last. So we project how far above or below the positional average a player sits, then put that deviation back on this year's actual average. We never have to forecast the level of a patch, which is the part nobody can do.
  • Shrink that gap toward zero by an amount measured on past events. If a player was 20% above average in the run-up, they will not be 20% above average at TI, they will be some fraction of that. The fraction is fitted on what actually happened at TI 2022 through 2025, per category, rather than guessed.
  • Hold everything out. The model that predicts TI 2024 is trained only on 2022, 2023 and 2025. Four events, 360 player-years, 103,109 player-game rows. Every accuracy number in this script is out-of-sample, meaning the model had never seen the answer.

Two words you need, and then the jargon is over

Concordance is the honest scoring metric for picking players. Show the model two supports from the same event and ask which one will score more. Concordance is how often it gets that right. 0.50 is a coin flip. 0.80 means it calls four out of five correctly. This is the number that matters when you are choosing between people.

R-squared is how much of the variation in a stat we can explain in advance, scored against a baseline of simply knowing the player's position. Zero means we add nothing over "they are a support, so here is the average support number". Above about 0.3 means the run-up is genuinely telling us something.

Those two can disagree, and when they do it is informative. A category can be easy to rank and hard to level, meaning we know who is better but not by how much.

The single most important line in Figure 2

Teamfight Participation is the highest scoring Green emblem at every position, worth around 1,400 points a game, and its level is extremely predictable: our average error on the level is 6%. But its ranking concordance is 0.61, barely above a coin flip. That combination means you should absolutely put it in your Green slot for the points, and you should never use it to choose between players. Everyone's Teamfight Participation is about the same.

Two numbers, four kinds of stat, four different things to do about it

Teamfight Participation is not a freak case, it is one corner of a pattern. Once you have both numbers for every emblem you can put them on two axes and the whole board sorts itself into four groups, and each group wants a different decision from you.

Top left, you can tell players apart and the number turns up. Watchers, Wards Placed, Camps Stacked, GPM, Creep Score, Deaths. Just pick the best player available. This is the boring, reliable half of the contest and it is where most of the settled slots live.

Top right is where the contest is actually won. Real separation between players and a huge spread in what they end up scoring. You get to choose, and the thing you are choosing between has an enormous ceiling. Build your roster around these first.

Bottom left, everyone is the same. The points are dead reliable, but the gap between the best player and the worst is smaller than our own measurement error, so there is nothing to choose. Take whoever is near the top and spend the decision somewhere else. That is Teamfight Participation exactly.

Bottom right, there is no pick to make. Roshan Kills, First Blood, Stuns. We cannot rank them and the outcome is a lottery on top of that. Do not spend a reroll or a roster decision here, and do not go hunting a weaker team for extra games either. Segment 5 has the numbers: the strongest teams in the run-up played 21.5 games at the event against 15.2 for the weakest, so the volume lever points at good teams, not at middling ones.

That pooled chart is the one to open with, but it is not the one you draft from. A banner slot belongs to a position, and both axes move when you split the field — sometimes enough to send an emblem clean across the board. Mid uses smokes so rarely that the whole category is worth 36 points a game there, so our error on it is larger than the number itself. Support Tormentor Kills goes from junk to the second-best call on the board.

The answer, before we explain any of it

If you only take one thing from this script, take this. Six emblem slots, three of them decided beyond argument, three of them genuinely undecidable, and the whole player-selection edge concentrated in one place.

What that table means when you say it out loud

Three of the six slot decisions are settled and nobody should spend a reroll token arguing with them. Runes in the Mid Blue slot wins by roughly a thousand points a game over anything else. Teamfight Participation wins the Green slot for the Mid and for the Support by more than a thousand points a game. Those are not close.

Three of them are genuinely undecidable, and this is the useful part. At Core and at Mid, the Red slot is a three-way tie between Creep Score, GPM and Deaths. The gaps between them are 19 to 192 points a game against measurement errors of 99 to 264 points. In practice, whatever the reroll hands you among those three, keep it.

The Support Blue slot is the second-closest call. Watchers leads Wards Placed by 257 points against an error of 206, so we are about 89% confident, which is a real lean and not a certainty. Then Smokes Used sits inside the error of Wards Placed. So the top of that colour is a scrum.

And the closest live call in the entire contest is the Core Green slot. Teamfight Participation leads Tormentor Kills 1,320 to 763, which sounds decisive, but the measured error on that comparison is 508 points, so we are only 86% confident. Teamfight Participation stays the pick, but if a reroll hands you Tormentor at Core, keep it, because Tormentor carries seven times the player-selection edge.

The backtest agrees with the theory, which is rare and reassuring

Running the whole pipeline as a forecast at each of the four past events picked the right emblem 23 times out of 28, and all five misses were a Red slot. That is exactly the cluster the theory flags as inseparable. Where the method says it can choose, it chose correctly at every event. Where it says it cannot, it missed, and the misses cost an average of 12 points a game on banners worth about 4,100.

The same test, run on every slot instead of the nine closest calls

Figure 6 takes the pairs worth arguing about. These three take the whole board, one role at a time: every emblem the slot can hold, ordered by what it pays, each carrying the error bar on the comparison against the emblem we are recommending. The bar is drawn as a percentage of that emblem's own points, so a wide bar means the number itself is soft, and any bar that reaches the dashed line is an emblem we cannot show is worse than the pick.

Why the bars are anchored to the pick rather than drawn independently

Two emblems are scored on the same player in the same games, so the patch, the game length and how the team ran cancel out of the difference between them. That is why a 96-point gap can be meaningful against emblems whose own levels we only know to within a few hundred points. An independent error bar per emblem would be roughly twice as wide and every emblem would overlap every other one, which is not what the four past events actually show. So every bar here answers one specific question: is this emblem worse than the pick?

Now the same question about people, which is where it gets uncomfortable

Everything above is about which stat goes in a slot. The other half of the contest is who you point it at, and concordance is the honest measure of whether we can. It has a direct visual: order the field by projection and cut the ladder wherever the gap to the next one clears the measured error on a head-to-head difference. Inside a cut, we are guessing.

The unit below is the one the contest actually hands you: a team's Core pair, pos 1 and pos 3 together, and its Support pair, pos 4 and pos 5 together, scored as the average of the two seats. Mid is one seat, so Mid is one player. That matters more than it sounds, and the reason is in the next section.

The pattern is stark and it is not about how many points the emblem pays. Support Watchers splits the sixteen support pairs into four tiers across 833 points a game. Teamfight Participation — the highest-paying Green emblem in every role, worth around 1,400 points a game — is one tier at Support and at Mid, and at Core it splits in two only by a single point of margin: 130 points from best pair to worst against an error of 129. The entire field fits inside one error bar.

Why pairing changes the answer, and not always in your favour

Averaging two seats should make a projection steadier, and for most emblems it does: the error on Support Watchers drops from around 300 points per player to 226 on the pair while the field still spans 833, which is why it is the cleanest read on the board.

Two emblems go the other way, and it is worth knowing why. Wards Placed and Smokes Used are jobs, and inside a team the two supports split them: across the 2026 field the pos 4 and pos 5 projections correlate at −0.89 on wards and −0.87 on smokes. Picking the right individual warder is very doable — 0.80 concordance at pos 4 — but the pair total barely moves between teams, so at the level the banner scores, Support Wards falls to 0.62 concordance over a 205-point spread. The ward duty moves around inside the team; it does not move between them.

03
Ranking
One stat, then three
ON SCREEN: Figure 11 heat map, let it breathe. Then Figure 12 scatter.

There is no best team. There is only best at this one stat

This is the finding that changes how you play the contest, and it is the one that surprises people most. Ranking the 16 teams gives an almost completely different order for every single emblem.

Here is how to measure that. Take the 16 teams. Rank them by expected Creep Score. Now rank them by expected Roshan kills. If there were such a thing as a generally strong core duo, those two lists would look similar. The statistic that measures "do these two lists agree" is a rank correlation, where 1.00 means identical order and 0.00 means no relationship at all.

The median rank correlation between two categories inside the same role is 0.17 for Core, 0.03 for Mid, and 0.12 for Support. That is essentially nothing. Knowing a team is first in one category tells you almost literally nothing about where they land in another.

So how do you use ranking across three stats at once?

Your banner is three emblems on one slot, and they are all scored in the same games by the same people. So the right question is not "who is best at Creep Score", it is "which team gives me the best sum of these three specific things".

Two rules make that tractable.

  • Rank on the sum, not on the parts. Add the three projected numbers up per team and rank that. Do not pick the team that leads one of your three categories and hope. The heat map is exactly what that hope looks like when it fails.
  • Weight the decision by where the ranking is actually reliable. A category where the run-up orders teams at 0.80 concordance deserves your attention. A category at 0.50 does not, no matter how big the point spread looks, because you cannot tell who is going to get it.

Multiplying those two together gives the map below, and it is the single most actionable chart in the script. Horizontal axis is how reliably we can order teams in that category. Vertical axis is how many points the choice is worth. Top right is where a decision both matters and is knowable. Bottom left is noise wearing a costume.

The short version of Figure 12

Spend your certainty on the Support slot. It has the widest field, best to worst is 1.33 times, against 1.13 to 1.15 times for the other two. It has the highest banner concordance at 0.74, and the largest edge at 1,053 points a series. LGD Gaming's support pair leads it, with OG second.

Treat Core and Mid as close to a coin flip on the whole banner. A 4% edge over a middling team at 0.61 concordance is not worth agonising over. Pick those two on individual categories instead, where both the spread and the reliability are far bigger: Mid Wards at 0.77 concordance, Mid Lotuses worth 1,095 a series, Mid Camps worth 491, Core Couriers at 0.68.

Do not spend any effort at all on Support Stuns, Mid or Core First Blood, Core Stuns, Support Roshan or Mid Couriers. Every one of them sits between 0.50 and 0.58 concordance. Either everyone is the same or we cannot tell who is not.

One more thing about combining three stats: they are not independent

A core who has a huge farming game also has a huge creep score and a huge madstone count. It is one good game showing up three times. The measured within-game correlation between Creep Score and Madstone for a core is 0.74, and for supports, Wards and Smokes correlate at 0.83, which is basically one behaviour wearing three names.

This matters directly and it costs money if you ignore it. If you assume your three emblems are independent and ask "what is the chance my Support clears all three of these thresholds in one game", you will understate the answer by 1.5 to 4.6 percentage points every time, because nearly every pair is positively correlated. The calculator in segment 6 handles this properly, and it will show you the wrong answer alongside the right one so you can see the size of the mistake.

The flip side is a real strategic warning. Three emblems that all correlate at 0.6 or higher are not a diversified banner. They are one bet made three times. If that game does not happen, none of them pay.

Team pace
Who plays long games
ON SCREEN: Figure 13 pace board, hold on it. Then the confidence table, then the pairing grid.

Which teams play long games, and how sure you are allowed to be

Every emblem is a count, and counts happen over time, so the length of the games your players are in sets the level of almost everything you score. That makes pace a team-selection question, not an emblem one: same player, slower team, more points. This segment answers which of the sixteen are slow, how confident that call is, and which specific matchups to expect to run long.

The board

The model is a two-way ridge. Game length is written as a field average plus a number for each of the two teams in it, so a team is not credited for its opponents being slow. It is fitted on the eighteen-month run-up with a 180-day half-life, frozen the day before TI 2026, and every team is shrunk toward the field mean in proportion to how few games it has. The number below is minutes this roster adds to a game, opponent held fixed.

Team Falcons are the slowest side in the field by a clear margin, at two minutes added to every game they are in. OG, LGD, Team Spirit, BoomBoys, Vici, Xtreme and HULIGANI follow, all of them adding around a minute. At the other end Aurora Gaming, Nigma Galaxy and GamerLegion each take about a minute off.

Those are the only calls the data supports. The bootstrap keeps eight teams on the slow side of the field in at least 95% of resamples and three on the fast side, and the five in the middle — Iron Wing, Yandex, Resilience, Liquid, TEAM VISION — never resolve. Do not read the ordering inside a group; read the group.

How much of a real prediction is this

The board is only worth quoting if the same method, frozen and applied forward, actually calls long games. So it was backtested the hard way: refit every month on the trailing eighteen months, predict the month that follows, never see its own game. That is 27,288 out-of-sample pro matches from 2019 to now.

Read those two facts together, because they are the whole segment. A single game is overwhelmingly not predictable: pace explains about one percent of how long any one match runs. But the tails are predictable. Take the fifth of TI pairings the model calls slowest and 35% of them ran past 45 minutes. Take the fastest fifth and 23% did. Same tournament, same format, a fifty percent higher chance of a long game purely from who is playing.

That is the honest shape of it. You cannot call a game. You can tilt a season.

The confidence table, which is the thing to quote

Every out-of-sample TI game, bucketed by how much pace the two teams add between them, against what actually happened. This is the table to read a specific matchup off.

The 2026 pairings

Put the board and the observed residual spread together and every one of the 120 possible matchups gets an expected length. The base is 43.2 minutes: the run-up averaged 40.6 and the four past TIs ran 6.4% longer than their own run-up, which is the level shift the projections already assume.

What the pace edge is worth in points

The conversion is the elasticity, which is how steeply a category scales with the clock. Creep Score is +1.25, so it grows slightly faster than the game does. Watchers, Runes and Lotuses sit near or above 1.0. GPM is +0.20 because it is already per-minute. Deaths is −0.72, because the emblem pays 1,950 minus 195 per death, so a longer game with more deaths pays less. Moving one seat from the fastest roster in the field to the slowest, opponent held fixed, is a 2.9-minute swing.

Do not add this on top of the projections — it is already inside them

A player's run-up average was earned in his own team's games, so it already contains his team's pace. Adding a pace term to the projection model explicitly changes accuracy by between +0.001 and −0.024 R-squared, which is nothing or slightly worse, on all eighteen categories. Use the board to break a tie between two players the projections cannot separate, and to know which way a matchup will drift. Do not add 92 points to anybody.

The limits, stated plainly

  • The slow group and the fast group are real. Eight teams and three teams respectively, at 95% bootstrap agreement, on 315 to 971 run-up games each.
  • Pace persists. A team's pace edge measured today still correlates 0.34 to 0.38 with its pace 60, 120, 240 and 365 days later. It is a stable trait, not a streak.
  • It does not carry to the team level at every event. Across 71 team-events at the four past TIs, pre-event pace correlates 0.28 with how long that team's games actually ran at the event. Per event: +0.31 in 2022, +0.58 in 2023, −0.09 in 2024, +0.36 in 2025. One event in four, the board was worth nothing.
  • You cannot call a single game. One percent of the variance. Anyone who tells you a specific match will go long is quoting a 0.30 probability against a base rate of 0.27 and calling it a read.
  • And you cannot call the event's own level. The four past TIs ran +6.5%, +9.7%, −2.3% and +11.8% against their own run-up. That average is already in the projections, and nothing in the run-up says which of the four this year will be.
05
More games
Or a better team
ON SCREEN: Figure 19 series-count bars. This is the counterintuitive one, slow down. Then Figure 20, per emblem.

Under this scoring rule, extra games are worth much less than you think

This is the question everyone asks: should I take a star player on a team that might go out early, or a solid player on a mid-table team that grinds out more series? The answer depends entirely on the payout rule, and under the rule we are using it is clear.

First, what the format actually allows

TI 2026's group stage is a Swiss of best-of-three series. A team plays until it has four series wins, which advances it, or four series losses, which eliminates it, capped at six series. So the entire field plays somewhere between four and six series. That is the whole range.

And here is the part that catches people out. A team that goes 4 and 0 plays the minimum four series. A team that goes 0 and 4 also plays four. The teams that play six are the ones grinding through the middle at 3 and 3. So "stronger team, fewer games" is not a hypothetical trade-off, it is literally what dominance looks like in a Swiss.

Second, what an extra series is actually worth

Because we score one series of two games, playing more series only helps in one way: it gives you more chances for a big series to show up, and we keep the best one. That is a maximum, and maxima grow slowly. Here is the measurement, run directly on each team's own projected game distribution.

So look at those two numbers side by side. Playing two extra series, the entire difference between a 4 and 0 run and a six-series grind, is worth about 4%. Picking the best support slot instead of the worst is worth about 36%.

The extra series is worth roughly the same as the entire Core team decision, which we already said was barely a decision. It is worth about a tenth of the Support decision.

The answer

Take the better slot. Do not chase series count. Under a rule that keeps one series of two games, the number of series a team plays is a small multiplier on a maximum, and the Swiss format caps it at four to six anyway. The quality of the slot is a direct multiplier on every game.

Two caveats that keep this honest. First, a team that gets knocked out entirely before the stage completes plays nothing, so avoiding a genuine disaster still matters. Second, if you carry into stage 2 and the contest sums across a deep run, this answer reverses completely: the run-up win rate predicts games played at 0.414 correlation, the top quartile of teams plays 21.5 games against 15.2 for the bottom, and a six-game difference on a 4,600-point banner is roughly 28,000 points, which dwarfs every emblem decision in this script.

But the banner average hides a fifteen-fold spread

That +3.4% to +4.4% is a blend of three emblems, and it should not be. Keeping a maximum only pays if the distribution has a tail to reach into. A First Blood or a Roshan kill is a rare event that either happens or does not; GPM is the same number every game. So extra series ought to be worth far more to the spiky emblems than to the steady ones, and they are.

The part that stops this being a strategy

You never field an emblem on its own. When the banner total chooses which series is kept, a spiky emblem is being selected on somebody else's number, and it keeps its upside only to the extent it moves with the rest of the banner. That capture rate averages 0.79 for Core, 0.71 for Support and 0.66 for Mid, and it collapses exactly where the emblem is uncorrelated with its neighbours: Support Roshan Kills keeps 0.47 of its theoretical gain, Mid Smokes Used 0.33.

Which is why the section's answer does not change. Series count is not something you buy — the Swiss caps the field at four to six and the teams that play six are the ones going 3-3. The one place it earns a decision is as a tiebreaker: in the Core Green slot, Teamfight Participation leads Tormentor Kills by 15% at four series and by only 5% at six, so the case for the safe pick is at its weakest for a team that grinds. Everywhere else the ordering inside a colour slot holds at every series count the format allows.

Why the third game of a series barely matters either

Same logic, one level down. A best-of-three keeps your best two games out of three instead of both games out of two. That extra draw is only worth 7% to 11% at banner level, because the banner sums are steady and the extra game rarely beats the two you already had.

But it is worth much more on the spiky emblems. Mid First Blood gains 50%, Support Roshan gains 50%, Mid Smokes gains 50%, against GPM and Teamfight Participation which gain 6%. So there is a real strategic rule hiding here: if you expect long series, spiky emblems gain roughly twice as much as steady ones.

The calculator
Live tool
ON SCREEN: hand over to the tool. Demo the default, then change one team.

Now build the roster and read the probability off the screen

Everything above is the reasoning. This is the instrument. Pick a team for each of the three banners, say how many series that team plays, pick the emblems inside each one, set a multiplier on anything you have a view about, name a score you want to hit, and it tells you how likely you are to hit it.

It opens on stage 2

The tool has two states, and the toggle at the top of it switches between them. Stage 1 is the group-stage calculator exactly as published before the event: sixteen teams, three emblems a banner, ladders built on the 548-day run-up alone. Stage 2 is the live one and the default. Three things change.

The field is eight teams. Iron Wing, Team Spirit, TEAM VISION, BoomBoys, Team Liquid, Team Yandex, Nigma Galaxy and Team Falcons are what is left in the bracket, so those are the only teams you can field.

Each banner is five emblems, not three. Per the rules sheet the stage-2 layouts are Core Red–Green–Red–Green–Red, Mid Red–Blue–Green–Red–Green and Support Blue–Green–Blue–Green–Blue. Your first three emblems carry over from stage 1 and two are added — the two added slots are the ones ringed in brass.

The model has seen the event. The per-game ladders are rebuilt on a pool that now contains all 109 TI 2026 games played so far (97 Swiss + 12 elimination). They enter as ordinary run-up games under the same 45-day half-life, which at ~0-14 days old gives them roughly 0.85–1.00 weight against 0.06 for a game six months back — 28% of the total weight in the pool from 3.6% of the games. The run-up → event level shift is applied only to the run-up half of each pool, since the TI games are already at event level. Scoring is on the calibration read off Valve's own per-player fantasy fields in the replays: Watchers ÷ 1.50, Lotuses ÷ 1.34, Tormentor Kills × 3.17 rather than × 2.2, and a Madstone bundle worth 3.18 stones rather than 2.7.

Two of those change a slot. Support Blue flips to Wards Placed (1,093 points a game against Watchers' 848) — the watcher field was running 50% hot, and correcting it undoes the flip the 4 August correction caused. Core Green's second slot goes to Tormentor Kills (963), which the honest ×3.17 lifts clear of Roshan Kills' 480. The stage-2 payout rule is assumed to be the group stage's — best two games of a series, best two series — because Valve has published nothing different for the bracket.

How it works underneath

For each banner it simulates 20,000 versions of the whole stage. A stage is the number of series you set — Swiss runs to four wins or four losses, so 4, 5 or 6 with 5 typical; an eight-team bracket runs 2 to 6, with 3 typical. Each series is a Bo3, going the distance 40.7% of the time, which is the observed rate at TI. Each game draws all of your emblems together, using the measured within-game correlations, so a good farming game correctly produces a good creep score and a good GPM at the same time. The draws come from a 104-level ladder of that specific team's projected per-game distribution, built from the games behind it and shifted to TI levels.

Then the payout rule bites twice. Each series keeps only its best two games, and the stage keeps only your best series. That is why the series count is a real lever and not bookkeeping: a sixth series never lowers your score, it only gives the best pair another chance to be beaten upward. Add the three banners, count how often the total clears your target.

The multiplier is a straight percentage on that emblem's points, which is where you put emblem quality, traits, the title bonus, or simply your own opinion. 100% is the study's baseline.

Five things to try with it

Set every banner's team to the one you think goes deepest, then move the series count rather than the team. In stage 2 the difference between two and five series is worth more than every emblem on the banner put together, and it is the one input you actually have a view on: it is a bracket prediction, not a stat prediction.

Change only the Support team and watch the whole distribution move. Then change only the Core team and watch almost nothing happen. That is segment 3 in one gesture.

Put Tormentor Kills in the Core Green slot and look at what happens to the shape rather than the average. The mean barely moves, the right tail gets much fatter, and the chance of a very high total goes up while the chance of a merely decent one goes down.

Drop one banner's team from five series to four and put another up to six. Nothing about the players changed, but the stage the payout is drawn from did, and the headline probability moves — that is segment 5's volume argument made concrete.

Set every multiplier to 108% to approximate the title bonus from segment 8, and note that it moves the probability of clearing a stretch target more than most of the emblem choices do.

Seven more things this study found that will change how you play

1. The single largest decision in the contest is which support you field

The gap between the best and worst support on the recommended banner is 3,152 points a game. That is larger than every emblem decision in this document combined. Watchers alone splits the support field into four clean tiers spanning 1,877 points a game: Thiolicor, Tims, Whitemon and Sneyking at the top, and Cr1t- and "not me" at the bottom on 712 and 624. Both of those two are excellent players, they simply do not take watchers.

2. Inside a tier, the ordering is not real

Those top four supports are a defensible group. Ordering them against each other is not supported by the data. Same at Core: the Creep Score tiers look like a skill ranking and are almost entirely position 1 against position 3. Read that table as "take a carry, not an offlaner", not as a ranking of carries.

3. The Mid slot is not worth optimising

The entire mid field is about 330 points a game wide on the recommended banner, and the uncertainty band on any single mid is about 1,100 points wide. No mid can be separated from any other. Pick the one you like and move on.

4. Green and Mid-Blue slots are big points that give away no edge

Once every serious entrant lands Teamfight Participation and Runes, those slots are identical across the whole field. They are worth a lot of points and zero differentiation. The entire player-selection edge lives in the Support Blue slot and, distantly, in the Red slots.

5. Steady stats predict averages. Habit stats predict ceilings

This one inverts everything above and it is genuinely useful. When you score the ceiling rather than the average, the metronome stats collapse and the countable habits survive. Core Teamfight Participation drops to 0.51 concordance at the ceiling, because everyone's ceiling is the same. But Support Tormentors holds 0.76, Mid Wards 0.74, Mid Smokes 0.72, Core Couriers 0.72. If your contest pays a ceiling, chase habits, not averages.

6. Opponents barely matter, and matter least where you care

A one standard deviation change in opponent strength moves a banner by 1.5% at Core, 4.6% at Mid, 2.7% at Support. More useful: Teamfight Participation is statistically immune at all three positions, because it is a ratio to your own team's kills, so a suppressive opponent shrinks the top and bottom of the fraction together. Wards Placed is also immune. Between them, those two fill four of the six recommended slots. Do not build an opponent adjustment table. The matchup specific effect is about 1.6% of a banner measured across roughly 800 cells with a median of nine games each, which is noise dressed as precision.

The one exception worth knowing: for a Mid, per game, facing the hardest rather than the softest of the 16 teams is worth slightly more than the entire spread between the best and worst mid on the board. Runes at 11% and Watchers at 9% are the two recommended emblems an opponent genuinely denies. Over a whole Swiss stage it cancels, because everyone faces the same field.

7. Run-up dispersion tells you nothing that run-up level does not

We tested whether a team's volatility in the run-up predicts their ceiling at the event, over and above their average. It adds 0.007 concordance across 45 categories, which is nothing. Where the simple level predictor gets a pair in the wrong order, the volatility-aware version rescues it 17% of the time. Volatility on its own is actually inverted: spiky teams tend to be low-average teams. There is no free variance signal to harvest.

The honest limits

  • Only 19 of 72 historical team-years played any best-of-three in the group stage, so the three-game arithmetic rests on resampling rather than observed behaviour. TI 2026 is the first best-of-three group stage.
  • Levels for Mid Smokes, Mid First Blood and Support Tormentors are unreliable, with fitted shifts of 1.37 to 1.84 and negative held-out R-squared. Use those three to order teams, not to size a total.
  • Some pools are thin. Team Resilience carries 24 to 28 effective run-up games per slot and OG's core carries 28, so their distribution tails in particular are under-determined. Aurora Gaming at 137 to 144 and TEAM VISION at 124 are the best covered.
  • Core and Support pairs are re-coupled statistically rather than observed. Only 3,519 of 7,953 core slots in the window are games the pair actually played together. The reconstruction was checked against real paired games and lands within 0.3% on the mean and 1.7% at the 90th percentile, but it is a reconstruction.
  • Madstone and Tormentor Kills are read through proxy fields and scaled by fixed factors of 2.7 and 2.2. Those set levels only. No ranking, correlation or elasticity anywhere in this script depends on them.

The title is free, it is worth 6 to 8%, and almost nobody optimises it

The title costs no reroll tokens, applies to your entire roster, and can be changed freely between stages. That makes it the highest return per unit of effort in the whole contest, and it is the one thing here that depends on which specific players you fielded.

The prefix is a draft forecast, not a stat forecast

A prefix pays a flat percentage on games where your player is on a hero from its pool. So the thing to predict is not a stat, it is a draft: what share of your roster's fantasy points will be earned on pool heroes. And it has to be points-weighted, not game-weighted, because the bonus multiplies points. A hero somebody only brings out in low-scoring games is worth less than its game share suggests.

And now the part that answers "which players pair well together"

Prefix coverage varies enormously between players at the same position. Coverage runs from effectively zero to nearly 6% on the same prefix depending on who you field, which is a 250-fold spread. Every player touches every pool at least once over 18 months, but several sit within rounding distance of nothing.

Every team, every slot, every prefix

The two tables above are the field answer and one worked example. Here is the whole board. Because the banner slots are independent — segment 6 — you are choosing a core pair from one team, a mid from another and a support pair from a third, and each of those three choices drags a different prefix behind it. These grids say what each candidate slot is worth under each title, so you can read the prefix off the three teams you actually want, or go the other way and find the slots that pay for the title you want to run.

What the grids say that the field table cannot

The mid slot decides the title. Cerulean pays a mid between 1.09% (Team Falcons, Marl1ne) and 5.52% (Aurora, Mikoto) — a five-fold spread, and the widest single cell range anywhere on the board. Nothing at core or support moves that far. If you are running Cerulean, the mid is the reason; if your mid is Marl1ne, Cerulean is the worst title you can hold and Crimson at 3.52% is the best.

Core is nearly a wash, so let it follow. Every core pair's own best prefix pays between 2.00% (GamerLegion) and 2.68% (Team Resilience), and the field means across the eight pools span only 1.27% to 2.03%. Pick the core pair for its banner and accept whatever prefix it happens to like.

Support is where Heroic and Golden live. They are the two best support pools at 2.53% and 2.39% on average, and Otherworldly is close to unusable at support (0.88%) while being the second-best mid pool (2.86%). LGD's supports on Heroic at 3.68%, Spirit's at 3.29% and Aurora's at 3.23% are the three biggest single cells outside the mid grid.

The alignments that actually stack. Cerulean wants Aurora or HULIGANI mid and Team Resilience or Iron Wing core; Heroic wants LGD or Spirit support with Iron Wing core; Golden wants Falcons or Aurora core with almost any support and never a mid.

Which pairings actually work

Cerulean roster: anchor it on a Cerulean-heavy mid. Mikoto at 5.52% is the strongest single Cerulean player in the field, and mids play 30.7% of their games on that pool against 12.8% for cores. A Cerulean title without a Cerulean mid is wasted.

Heroic roster: anchor it on the supports. Supports play 28.5% of their games on the Heroic pool, and Cr1t- at 5.82% is the strongest Heroic player anywhere. That is also a genuinely useful pairing, because Cr1t- is the weakest support in the field on the Watchers emblem at 712 points. If you want him for another reason, Heroic is how you recover some of it.

Golden roster: anchor it on cores and supports, never on a mid. Mids play 8.9% of their games on Golden, the lowest coverage of any role and pool combination on the board.

Avoid entirely: Emerald and Royal are the only two prefixes it is clearly wrong to take at the field level. Everything else is inside the measurement error of everything else, and pays 1.7% to 2.2% regardless.

The suffix is simpler, because it is a rate, not a forecast

A suffix pays a percentage on games where a match-level event happens. So the number to know is how often it actually fires at a TI, and there are two traps waiting.

Two traps, one of them absolute

The Patient has never once paid out. It offers 23%, tied for the biggest bonus on the sheet, for a game where first blood does not happen until after 10 minutes. In 645 games of TI across four years, the latest first blood has ever occurred is 9 minutes 8 seconds. The condition is written for pub games. Its measured rate is zero, with a 95% upper bound of 0.6%.

The Decisive is the subtler one. 24% is the largest single bonus available and it is worth 0.66%, because short games are both rare at 4.3% and low scoring at 36% below average. The two effects compound.

The Clutch, and the one thing to check before you lock it

The Clutch pays 16% on the last possible game of a series. Pooled across all four events it is worth 4.94%, 1.7 times the next best option — but that pooled number blends two completely different regimes, and it depends on the group format.

In a best-of-two group stage, every second group game is a decider, so the Clutch fired on about 49% of group games at TI 2022, 2023 and 2024, and was worth 4.3% to 6.4%. TI 2025 ran best-of-three groups and it collapsed to 18.5%, worth 2.74%. TI 2026 runs best-of-three groups, so that 2.74% is the number to plan on, which is what Figure 25 now shows — and it lands just under the Underdog's 2.84% rather than clearly ahead of it. Essentially a tie between the two, not a standout pick, which is still worth taking since it costs nothing.

One note on the Underdog, which pays 6% on games your player loses. Its 50% rate is not an estimate, it is arithmetic: across a whole event exactly half of all player-games are losses. It is the only suffix whose rate is a choice, because picking a worse player raises it. Do not do that. A deep run is two to three times the games of an early exit, and losses score 5% below average anyway. Take the Underdog as a free 2.84% on the roster you already wanted, never as a reason to change the roster.
